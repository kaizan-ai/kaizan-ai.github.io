The story of the Daruma

Fall down seven times, get up eight.

That’s nana korobi ya oki, the Japanese proverb every Daruma carries. Give one a push and its weighted base rocks it straight back up. Here’s how a Zen monk became Japan’s favourite way to set a goal.

七転び八起き

  1. c. 500 AD

    The monk who sat for nine years

    Daruma is the Japanese name for Bodhidharma, the monk who brought Zen Buddhism to China. Legend says he sat facing a cave wall in meditation for nine years, until his arms and legs wasted away. That’s why the doll is round, with no arms or legs.

  2. 1600s–1700s

    The toy that always gets back up

    Self-righting toys called okiagari-koboshi, “little monks who get back up”, were popular around Kyoto and Osaka by the mid-1600s. Zen painters like Hakuin Ekaku painted Daruma again and again, and the two ideas met: a tough, unshakeable monk who can’t be knocked down.

  3. 1760s

    Takasaki and the first Daruma dolls

    At the Shorinzan Daruma Temple in Takasaki, north-west of Tokyo, the head priest Togaku carved wooden moulds so local people could make papier-mâché Daruma themselves and sell them as New Year charms. Takasaki still makes about 80% of Japan’s Daruma today.

  4. Today

    One eye now, one eye later

    People buy a Daruma with blank eyes at New Year. They set a goal and paint in its left eye, then paint the right eye when the goal is done. At the end of the year old Daruma go back to the temple to be thanked and burned, and a new one comes home.

Read the face

  • Eyebrows shaped like cranes, and a beard like a turtle’s shell. In Japan the crane is said to live a thousand years and the turtle ten thousand, so the face is a wish for a long life.
  • Red for the robe Bodhidharma wore. In Japan red is also thought to keep bad luck away.
  • Blank eyes until the owner gives it a goal. A Daruma with one eye still to paint is a goal in progress.

The Daruma holds the goal. Kaizen gets you there.

Kaizen is the Japanese idea that steady, everyday improvement beats the rare big overhaul.

改善

kaizen /ˈkaɪ.zɛn/

noun · from Japanese kai (change) + zen (good)

  1. Change for the better.
  2. Continuous improvement: big results from small changes, made every day by everyone.

  • Small steps, every day

    No big launches or once-a-year resets. A small improvement today, another tomorrow, and they add up.

  • Everyone improves

    It isn’t only for managers. Everyone, from the front line to the CEO, spots what could be better and fixes it.

  • Question what already works

    Good enough is a starting point. Even when things run well, there’s a small way to make them better.

  1. 1950sJapanese manufacturers rebuild after the war. Toyota makes daily, small improvement part of how its factories run.
  2. 1986Masaaki Imai’s book Kaizen takes the idea to the rest of the world.
  3. TodayKaizen is used everywhere from hospitals to software teams, wherever small changes can add up.
