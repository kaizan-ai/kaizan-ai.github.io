Agencies and consultancies are being asked to do more for the same fee.

Clients expect AI to make their partners faster and better. They’re starting to question fees built on headcount. And finance teams are capping AI spend until it proves itself.

So the pressure is on to protect and grow the revenue that’s already there. Yet most firms still judge the health of their client relationships the way they did ten years ago. That means gut feel, a few good conversations, and hoping everyone is doing a good job.

That was never good enough. It’s even less good enough now.

## The old signals arrive too late

Look at how most firms track their clients. There’s a RAG status in a spreadsheet, updated the night before the board meeting. There’s an NPS or CSAT survey once or twice a year. There’s an account plan that gets refreshed annually, if anyone remembers. And underneath all of it sits the account lead’s instinct.

By the time any of that turns red, the client has already made up their mind.

The signals were there much earlier. A frustrated comment on a call. A decision-maker who stopped replying to emails. A new market mentioned in passing on Slack. Replies getting slower. Fewer ideas coming back. A team talking to the same one contact every week while nobody more senior hears from them.

That’s how good relationships quietly turn into supplier relationships. And suppliers are easy to replace.

## More AI isn’t the same as better client intelligence

Most firms have already started with AI. They’ve got a note-taker on every call, and a few clever workflows someone built in Claude or ChatGPT.

These help, but they don’t solve the problem.

A note-taker only sees the calls it joins. It misses the emails, the messages and the delivery work, which is where most client relationships actually happen. In-house AI workflows have the opposite problem. Each one sits in its own silo, blind to what the others know.

General-purpose LLMs are brilliant at a lot of things, but they weren’t built for how client relationships work.

They can’t bring every conversation around a client together with the right permissions. A head of client services should see every message across an account. A junior account manager shouldn’t. They have no lasting memory of the client either: what the client is trying to achieve, which stakeholders matter, or what each of those people likes and dislikes. Every new chat starts from zero.

So the insight never builds up over time. One great meeting summary here, one clever prompt there, and none of it adds up to an agency brain.

None of these tools can answer the question that really matters: is this client drifting away, or ready to grow?

## What the best firms are doing now

The firms pulling ahead aren’t adding more tools. They’re building one client memory that everything else draws on.

That means bringing every conversation, email, message and piece of delivery work for each client into one secure place that’s always up to date. Each relationship then gets scored on that evidence, not on opinion. And the score is compared with what good looks like across similar firms.

Once that’s in place, the work changes completely.

Risks, delivery friction and growth opportunities show up early, with clear actions attached. Leaders can see which relationships need attention and which accounts can grow, without another round of catch-up meetings. Account teams walk into every conversation with the full picture. AI Helpers take on the routine account work, so the cost of serving each client goes down while the quality goes up.

The firm’s own AI gets better too, and this is the part most people miss. When every tool a team builds can draw on the same permissioned client intelligence, those tools stop working in silos. They start working from the truth.

That’s the real shift, from reactive account management to proactive account management. You stop finding out you’ve lost a client and start seeing it coming months in advance.
