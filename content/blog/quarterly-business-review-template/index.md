A quarterly business review template is the agenda and slide structure an agency uses to run a QBR: a relationship health check, the quarter's numbers against target, wins and risks, stakeholder coverage, and next quarter's agreed actions. Most quarterly business review templates online are generic slide galleries built for any industry. This one is built specifically for agencies and professional services firms.

## What key metrics a quarterly business review template contains

A good QBR follows the same eight-part structure, because the shape of a good QBR doesn't change much between firm types, even when the numbers on the slides do; the template helps present the review consistently, using relevant information for the audience. The deck runs:

1. **Relationship health snapshot.** A one-page RAG view across satisfaction, engagement, relationship depth and growth readiness.
2. **The quarter in numbers.** A scorecard of target versus actual, covering key metrics and quarterly performance, with a trend column.
3. **Wins and challenges.** What went well, and what needed attention, side by side.
4. **Stakeholder coverage.** Who's engaged, who's gone quiet, and who holds the budget.
5. **Risks and mitigations.** Named risks, likely impact, and what's being done about each one.
6. **Where this account can grow.** Growth signals and a suggested next step for each.
7. **Action items and next steps.** Owners and dates for next quarter, plus space for the client's own priorities.
8. **Appendix: agency metrics sheet.** A one-page reference the account team fills in before the meeting and keeps on file, including the performance data used in the review.

## How to use the QBR template, step by step

A quarterly business review template is only as good as what goes into it. Here's what each section is actually for, and what a good answer looks like.

### Relationship health snapshot

Rate each of the four boxes green, amber or red, and write one line explaining why. Not "amber because things are a bit quiet," but the specific thing that's true: *decision-maker hasn't been on a call in six weeks*, or *client flagged budget pressure twice this quarter*. The reason is the useful part because it helps identify the issue clearly. The colour is just a flag to the reader that a reason exists.

### The quarter in numbers

Pull these the morning of the review, not the week before. A stale number is the fastest way to lose a room that's checking your figures against their own dashboard in real time. Fill in target, actual, and a trend arrow against last quarter, not just this quarter in isolation, so the objective behind the numbers and overall performance are clear: a single number with no comparison tells the client nothing about direction.

Use charts only where they make changes in trend easier to read.

### Wins and challenges

Three wins, three challenges, each tied to measurable outcomes and backed by one sentence of detail. The challenges column is the one agencies are tempted to thin out. Don't. A QBR that only ever shows good news reads as curated, and clients notice. Kaizan's own research across more than a million agency client calls found that roughly 69% of accounts that later became genuinely at risk had raised a concern in conversation at least once before anyone treated it as a risk on paper, and about 38% raised the same concern more than once. The accounts that stayed are usually the ones where that kind of signal made it onto a slide instead of staying in a transcript nobody reread.

### Stakeholder coverage

List every stakeholder on your target map, even the ones who've gone quiet, and mark each one Engaged, Needs attention, or At risk based on when they last had a meaningful interaction with your team. This is the section most agencies fill in optimistically, listing who they'd like to be talking to rather than who they're actually talking to. Kaizan's benchmark data found that the gap between the strongest and weakest client relationships isn't usually about which decision-makers an account team has identified. Both groups map roughly the same stakeholders. The strongest accounts just get almost twice as many calls and emails with the actual decision-maker as the weakest ones. The fix is frequency, not a longer contact list.

### Risks and mitigations

Name the risk plainly, link it to the agreed goals, state its likely impact on the account in business terms, and write what's actually being done about it, with an owner. A clean risk register on an active account is worth questioning, not celebrating. It usually means a risk is being heard somewhere in the account and not written down, more often than it means nothing is wrong.

### Where this account can grow

Only fill this section in when the relationship can support it. A growth pitch dropped into a strained account reads as a sales call, not a partnership, and most clients can tell the difference instantly. For each opportunity, name the signal that triggered it (something the client actually said or did, not a guess) and a next step that becomes a concrete plan with an owner and timing, not just a proposal handoff. Save the proposal for the follow-up.

### Agreed actions

Every action needs an owner and a date, not a general sense of direction. Leave space on the slide for the client's own priorities for next quarter too, and write those down verbatim in the room rather than paraphrasing them afterward. A QBR that only records your own commitments is a status update with better formatting. Capture any unresolved open questions before the meeting ends.

## Common mistakes when filling in a QBR template

**Treating it as a status read-out.** The moment a quarterly business review becomes a recap of deliverables, it reads to the client as a vendor update, not a partnership conversation, when it should drive clear decisions about what changes next.

**Filling it in from memory the night before.** This is how a stakeholder map ends up optimistic, a risk register ends up empty, and a number ends up three weeks stale. If prep starts the evening before the meeting, it started too late; preparation starts earlier so the review can focus on the right evidence.

**Skipping the challenges column.** A quarter with zero named challenges isn't a strong quarter. It's a QBR that got edited for comfort before anyone saw it.

**Running one format for every account.** A top-tier account and a small retained client rarely need the same cadence or the same length of review, and the format should adapt to the client and the audience.

**Letting "where this account can grow" drift into a pitch.** The clients who feel pitched to in a QBR are the ones who start shopping the account around. Raise growth only when the relationship can carry it.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I fill in the quarterly business review template?**
Start with a qbr agenda, then gather the most relevant metrics and feedback, and fill each slide with evidence tied to the quarter's goals. Your quarterly business review presentation should be tailored to the audience and the client rather than copied from account to account. End with a final slide that captures key takeaways, next steps, and any action plan agreed in the room.

**How do I keep the QBR template current without redoing it from scratch every quarter?**
Keep the account plan updated between reviews so the next QBR starts from current information instead of being rebuilt from scratch. A simple one pager or single slide summary can be refreshed weekly to track company goals and revenue changes. Teams that prefer a presentation-based workflow can maintain it in PowerPoint or Google Slides.
