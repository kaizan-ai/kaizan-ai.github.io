A quarterly business review template is the agenda and slide structure an agency uses to run a QBR: a relationship health check, the quarter's numbers against target, wins and risks, stakeholder coverage, and next quarter's agreed actions. Most quarterly business review templates online are generic slide galleries built for any industry. This one is built specifically for agencies and professional services firms.

## What a quarterly business review template contains

A good QBR follows the same eight-part structure, because the shape of a good QBR doesn't change much between firm types, even when the numbers on the slides do. The deck runs:

1. **Relationship health snapshot.** A one-page RAG view across satisfaction, engagement, relationship depth and growth readiness.
2. **The quarter in numbers.** A scorecard of target versus actual, with a trend column.
3. **Wins and challenges.** What went well, and what needed attention, side by side.
4. **Stakeholder coverage.** Who's engaged, who's gone quiet, and who holds the budget.
5. **Risks and mitigations.** Named risks, likely impact, and what's being done about each one.
6. **Where this account can grow.** Expansion signals and a suggested next step for each.
7. **Agreed actions.** Owners and dates for next quarter, plus space for the client's own priorities.
8. **Appendix: agency metrics sheet.** A one-page reference the account team fills in before the meeting and keeps on file.

## How to use the QBR template, step by step

A quarterly business review template is only as good as what goes into it. Here's what each section is actually for, and what a good answer looks like.

### Relationship health snapshot

Rate each of the four boxes green, amber or red, and write one line explaining why. Not "amber because things are a bit quiet," but the specific thing that's true: *decision-maker hasn't been on a call in six weeks*, or *client flagged budget pressure twice this quarter*. The reason is the useful part. The color is just a flag to the reader that a reason exists.

### The quarter in numbers

Pull these the morning of the review, not the week before. A stale number is the fastest way to lose a room that's checking your figures against their own dashboard in real time. Fill in target, actual, and a trend arrow against last quarter, not just this quarter in isolation: a single number with no comparison tells the client nothing about direction.

### Wins and challenges

Three wins, three challenges, each with one sentence of detail. The challenges column is the one agencies are tempted to thin out. Don't. A QBR that only ever shows good news reads as curated, and clients notice. Kaizan's own research across more than a million agency client calls found that roughly 69% of accounts that later became genuinely at risk had raised a concern in conversation at least once before anyone treated it as a risk on paper, and about 38% raised the same concern more than once. The accounts that stayed are usually the ones where that kind of signal made it onto a slide instead of staying in a transcript nobody reread.

### Stakeholder coverage

List every stakeholder on your target map, even the ones who've gone quiet, and mark each one Engaged, Needs attention, or At risk based on when they last had a meaningful interaction with your team. This is the section most agencies fill in optimistically, listing who they'd like to be talking to rather than who they're actually talking to. Kaizan's benchmark data found that the gap between the strongest and weakest client relationships isn't usually about which decision-makers an account team has identified. Both groups map roughly the same stakeholders. The strongest accounts just get almost twice as many calls and emails with the actual decision-maker as the weakest ones. The fix is frequency, not a longer contact list.

### Risks and mitigations

Name the risk plainly, state its likely impact in business terms, and write what's actually being done about it, with an owner. A clean risk register on an active account is worth questioning, not celebrating. It usually means a risk is being heard somewhere in the account and not written down, more often than it means nothing is wrong.

### Where this account can grow

Only fill this section in when the relationship can support it. An expansion pitch dropped into a strained account reads as a sales call, not a partnership, and most clients can tell the difference instantly. For each opportunity, name the signal that triggered it (something the client actually said or did, not a guess) and a next step that's a conversation, not a proposal. Save the proposal for the follow-up.

### Agreed actions

Every action needs an owner and a date, not a general sense of direction. Leave space on the slide for the client's own priorities for next quarter too, and write those down verbatim in the room rather than paraphrasing them afterward. A QBR that only records your own commitments is a status update with better formatting.

## Common mistakes when filling in a QBR template

**Treating it as a status read-out.** The moment a quarterly business review becomes a recap of deliverables, it reads to the client as a vendor update, not a partnership conversation.

**Filling it in from memory the night before.** This is how a stakeholder map ends up optimistic, a risk register ends up empty, and a number ends up three weeks stale. If prep starts the evening before the meeting, it started too late.

**Skipping the challenges column.** A quarter with zero named challenges isn't a strong quarter. It's a QBR that got edited for comfort before anyone saw it.

**Running one format for every account.** A top-tier account and a small retained client rarely need the same cadence or the same length of review.

**Letting "where this account can grow" drift into a pitch.** The clients who feel pitched to in a QBR are the ones who start shopping the account around. Raise growth only when the relationship can carry it.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I fill in the quarterly business review template?**
Work through it in the order the slides are built: health snapshot, then the numbers, then wins and challenges, stakeholder coverage, risks, growth opportunities, and finally agreed actions. The walkthrough above explains what a good answer looks like for each section.

**How do I keep the QBR template current without redoing it from scratch every quarter?**
Build a standing weekly habit of logging what changed on your top accounts, or connect the account's real conversation history so the health snapshot, stakeholder table and risk register populate automatically rather than from memory. The section above on keeping it current covers both routes.
